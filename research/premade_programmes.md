## Glossary

- **To failure:** The last rep you can complete with full range and control. If you tried another, it would not go up.
- **To technical failure:** The last rep you can do with perfect form. Used on barbell lifts where grinding ugly reps is risky.
- **Drop set:** At failure, immediately reduce the weight by about 25% and go to failure again.
- **Myo-reps:** At failure, rest 5 slow breaths, do 3 to 5 more reps, repeat about 3 times.
- **Rest-pause:** At failure, rest about 15 seconds, then go to failure again.
- **Stretched half reps (lengthened partials):** After failure, keep doing half reps in the stretched half of the movement.
- **Mechanical drop:** At failure, switch straight to an easier version of the same movement and continue.
- **Superset (A1/A2):** Alternate the paired exercises, resting as listed between them.
- **Ramp-up sets:** Progressively heavier warm-up sets of a few reps that build to your working weight. Never to failure.

## Relentless PPL

*A six-day push, pull, legs split with two different versions of every session, built to grow everything twice a week.*

**Intermediate · Gym · 6 days a week · 6 weeks**

For intermediate lifters with at least a year of consistent training who can get to the gym six days a week. Each muscle is hit twice a week from two angles: the A sessions lean on stretch-focused presses and pulls for upper chest, lats and quads, the B sessions on heavy flat pressing, back thickness and the hamstrings and glutes. Main lifts stay heavy and clean, machines and cables carry the hard sets to failure, so you get plenty of growth stimulus without beating up your joints.

**Equipment:** barbell, rack, bench, dumbbells, cable, machines, pull-up bar, dip bars

**Machines used:** chest press, pec deck, shoulder press, hack squat, leg press, seated leg curl, lying leg curl, leg extension, reverse pec deck, preacher curl, standing calf, seated calf, hip adduction

**How hard:** Barbell presses, rows, pull-ups and RDLs stop at the last perfect rep. Machines, cables and isolation exercises go to genuine failure on every working set, with one intensity technique on the final set where listed.

**Weekly layout:** Push A / Pull A / Legs A / Push B / Pull B / Legs B / Rest

**Rotation:** Six distinct sessions run in the same order every week; effort ramps up across the block.

### Week by week

- **Week 1:** Calibration week. Run every session but stop each set with about 2 good reps left and skip intensity techniques. Log every weight and rep: this is your baseline.
- **Week 2:** Train as written. Sets marked to failure now go to failure. Intensity techniques stay off for one more week.
- **Week 3:** Full programme, intensity techniques on. Beat last week on every exercise by at least one rep or a small load jump.
- **Week 4:** Full programme, intensity techniques on. Beat last week on every exercise by at least one rep or a small load jump.
- **Week 5:** Full programme, intensity techniques on. Beat last week on every exercise by at least one rep or a small load jump.
- **Week 6:** Deload. Half the sets, same or slightly lighter weights, nothing near failure, no intensity techniques.

**Progression:** Double progression. Each exercise has a rep range. When you hit the top of the range on every working set, add the smallest load jump next session (2.5 kg on barbells and machines, 1 to 2 kg per dumbbell) and build the reps back up. On isolation work, add reps first and load second. If you miss the bottom of the range, keep the weight and beat it next time.

**Deload:** The final week is a deload: same sessions and exercises, half the sets, the same weights or 10% lighter, and every set stops with 3 or more reps left. No intensity techniques. Take an early deload if your main lifts go backwards two sessions in a row, sleep and motivation drop, or joints start to ache.

**Why it's built this way:** Hitting each muscle twice a week with about 12 to 18 hard sets sits in the zone where the Pelland et al. (2024) meta-regression shows growth still climbing before returns flatten. Isolation choices favour long muscle lengths, such as seated leg curls (Maeo et al. 2021), overhead triceps work (Maeo et al. 2023) and incline curls, because training muscles in a stretched position produces more growth. Failure is saved for machines and cables, where it is safe and repeatable, while barbell lifts stay crisp so fatigue does not leak into the next session.

## Hybrid Five

*Heavy upper and lower days followed by a high-output push, pull and legs block. Five days, strength and size in one week.*

**Intermediate · Gym · 5 days a week · 6 weeks**

For intermediate lifters who want to get stronger on the big lifts while still training like a bodybuilder. The first two days are lower-rep strength sessions built around the bench, squat, pull-up and RDL. After a rest day, push, pull and legs switch to moderate weights, higher reps and intensity techniques. Every muscle is trained twice a week with a different rep range each time, so you get the strength of a heavy day and the volume of a pump day without either one crowding out the other.

**Equipment:** barbell, rack, bench, dumbbells, cable, machines, pull-up bar

**Machines used:** chest press, hack squat, leg press, seated leg curl, lying leg curl, leg extension, reverse pec deck, standing calf, seated calf

**How hard:** Upper and Lower days: heavy, crisp sets that stop at the last perfect rep, with long rests. Push, Pull and Legs days: most sets go to failure, with drop sets, myo-reps and stretched half reps on the last set of isolation work.

**Weekly layout:** Upper (Strength) / Lower (Strength) / Rest / Push / Pull / Legs / Rest

**Rotation:** Five distinct sessions repeated weekly.

## Blueprint Upper/Lower

*Four days a week, every muscle twice, every session with a clear job. The split most lifters should run for years.*

**Intermediate · Gym · 4 days a week · 8 weeks**

For lifters with six months or more of training who want a schedule that fits around work and still builds serious muscle. Upper 1 is built around horizontal pressing and rowing, Upper 2 around vertical pulling and overhead pressing. Lower 1 is squat-led, Lower 2 is hinge-led. Rest days fall naturally between pairs, so recovery is rarely a problem.

**Equipment:** barbell, rack, bench, dumbbells, cable, machines, pull-up bar, dip bars

**Machines used:** chest press, hack squat, leg press, seated leg curl, lying leg curl, leg extension, reverse pec deck, standing calf, seated calf

**How hard:** The first lift of each day stops at the last perfect rep. Everything after it goes to failure on the final set, and isolation work goes to failure on every set.

**Weekly layout:** Upper 1 / Lower 1 / Rest / Upper 2 / Lower 2 / Rest / Rest

**Rotation:** Four distinct sessions repeated weekly. Weeks 1 to 3 build reps, weeks 4 to 7 push load, week 8 deloads.

### Week by week

- **Week 1:** Calibration. Stop every set with about 2 good reps left, no techniques. Log everything.
- **Week 2:** Train as written, techniques off. Aim to add reps everywhere.
- **Week 3:** Techniques on. Push reps to the top of each range.
- **Week 4:** Load week. Anything that hit the top of its range gets heavier.
- **Week 5:** Beat week 4 by a rep or a small jump in load on every exercise.
- **Week 6:** Beat week 5. Add one extra set to the first isolation exercise of each session if you are recovering well.
- **Week 7:** Peak week. Best performance of the block on the first lift of each day.
- **Week 8:** Deload. Half the sets, nothing near failure.

**Progression:** Double progression. Each exercise has a rep range. When you hit the top of the range on every working set, add the smallest load jump next session (2.5 kg on barbells and machines, 1 to 2 kg per dumbbell) and build the reps back up. On isolation work, add reps first and load second. If you miss the bottom of the range, keep the weight and beat it next time.

**Deload:** The final week is a deload: same sessions and exercises, half the sets, the same weights or 10% lighter, and every set stops with 3 or more reps left. No intensity techniques. Take an early deload if your main lifts go backwards two sessions in a row, sleep and motivation drop, or joints start to ache.

**Why it's built this way:** Training each muscle twice a week outperforms once a week for growth when weekly sets are not matched (Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger 2016), and four days is the simplest way to get there. Putting the heavy compound first while you are fresh and finishing with stretch-biased isolation work gets both strength and size from each session. A planned deload after seven hard weeks clears fatigue before it stalls progress.

- **Week 1:** Week A. Calibration: stop every set with about 2 good reps left, skip techniques, log everything.
- **Week 2:** Week B. Calibration for the B sessions: stop every set with about 2 good reps left, log everything.
- **Week 3:** Week A. Full effort and intensity techniques. Beat the numbers from the last time you ran this exact session.
- **Week 4:** Week B. Full effort and intensity techniques. Beat the numbers from the last time you ran this exact session.
- **Week 5:** Week A. Full effort and intensity techniques. Beat the numbers from the last time you ran this exact session.
- **Week 6:** Week B. Full effort and intensity techniques. Beat the numbers from the last time you ran this exact session.
- **Week 7:** Week A. Full effort and intensity techniques. Beat the numbers from the last time you ran this exact session.
- **Week 8:** Week B, deload. Half the sets, same or slightly lighter weights, nothing near failure.

**Progression:** Compare each session with the last time you ran that same version, two weeks earlier. Double progression. Each exercise has a rep range. When you hit the top of the range on every working set, add the smallest load jump next session (2.5 kg on barbells and machines, 1 to 2 kg per dumbbell) and build the reps back up. On isolation work, add reps first and load second. If you miss the bottom of the range, keep the weight and beat it next time.

**Deload:** The final week is a deload: same sessions and exercises, half the sets, the same weights or 10% lighter, and every set stops with 3 or more reps left. No intensity techniques. Take an early deload if your main lifts go backwards two sessions in a row, sleep and motivation drop, or joints start to ache.

**Why it's built this way:** When weekly sets are matched, training a muscle once or twice a week grows it similarly (Pelland et al. 2024), so a body-part split works as long as each day carries enough quality sets. Each session keeps a muscle to roughly 10 to 12 hard sets, the point where more work in one sitting mostly adds fatigue. Alternating heavy and pump weeks trains each muscle across the full rep spectrum and gives tendons a lighter week every other week.

**Deload:** Week 8 is either a deload (half the sets, 70% of top set weights) or a test week. Start the next block with top sets about 5% heavier than week 1 of this one.

One top set plus back-offs gives heavy practice without the fatigue of many maximal sets, leaving room for hypertrophy work. Bigger muscles have more potential to produce force, so the bodybuilding work is part of the strength plan rather than a distraction.

Pre-fatigue isolation work and stretch-focused exercise choices make every working set count.

### Week by week

- **Week 1:** Calibration week. Run every session but stop each set with about 2 good reps left and skip intensity techniques. Log every weight and rep: this is your baseline.
- **Week 2:** Train as written. Sets marked to failure now go to failure. Intensity techniques stay off for one more week.
- **Week 3:** Full programme, intensity techniques on. Beat last week on every exercise by at least one rep or a small load jump.

- **Week 7:** Full programme, intensity techniques on. Beat last week on every exercise by at least one rep or a small load jump.
- **Week 8:** Deload. Half the sets, same or slightly lighter weights, nothing near failure, no intensity techniques.

**Progression:** Variation ladder. When you hit the top of the rep range on every set, move to the next harder version or add weight in a backpack, then build the reps back up. Examples: push-up to deficit push-up to archer push-up; band-assisted pull-up to pull-up to weighted pull-up; box pistol to lower box to full pistol.

**Deload:** The final week is a deload: same sessions and exercises, half the sets, the same weights or 10% lighter, and every set stops with 3 or more reps left. No intensity techniques. Take an early deload if your main lifts go backwards two sessions in a row, sleep and motivation drop, or joints start to ache.

**Why it's built this way:** Push-ups build chest and triceps as well as the bench press when effort and load are matched (Kikuchi and Nakazato 2017), and light loads grow muscle when taken close to failure. The variation ladder is how bodyweight training applies progressive overload. Exercises like Nordic curls, sissy squats and deficit push-ups load muscles in deep stretch, which tends to produce more growth.
