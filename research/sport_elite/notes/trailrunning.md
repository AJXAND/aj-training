# Trail and fell running: research note

Scope: the app has a separate running planner, so this covers gym, prevention and terrain conditioning only.

## Demands
- Vernillo et al. 2017 (Sports Med 47(4):615-629): uphill running cost rises linearly with gradient and needs more positive work, especially at the hip. Downhill running is dominated by energy absorption (eccentric work), with longer flight time and lower step frequency; energy cost is lowest around -20% and rises beyond it.
- Lemire et al. 2021 (J Sci Med Sport), 5 km time-trials at +/-8%: uphill performance predicted by velocity at VO2max, BMI and maximal knee extensor strength (r2 0.94); downhill by velocity at VO2max, leg musculotendinous stiffness and maximal extensor strength (r2 0.84). Strength matters both ways.
- Bontemps et al. 2020 (Sports Med 50:2083-2110): downhill running causes exercise-induced muscle damage (strength loss, soreness, CK rise), driven by slope, duration and speed. Runners with least downhill experience get the most damage. Prior downhill exposure is the most effective protective strategy found so far; compression and footwear are unproven.
- Strength training in distance runners improves economy and time-trial performance (Blagrove et al. 2018; Balsalobre-Fernandez et al. 2016: resistance plus plyometrics, 2-3 a week, 8-12 weeks). No trail-specific strength RCT found; evidence borrowed from road runners, graded moderate.

## Injuries
- Viljoen et al. 2021 (Sports Med 51:917-943), 16 studies, 8644 runners: injuries 1.6 to 4285 per 1000 h (very wide, mostly race studies). Foot most common, then knee, lower leg, thigh, ankle. Types: skin lacerations and abrasions (falls), blisters, muscle strains, cramp, ligament sprains. Illness mostly gastrointestinal.
- Viljoen et al. 2022 (BJSM 56(10):577-587), living review of risk factors: 0.7 to 61.2 injuries per 1000 running hours. Risk factors with some evidence: more experience, faster level, accident proneness, neglecting warm-up, no structured plan, training on asphalt, two sessions a day. Evidence base small.
- Practice: falls and ankle sprains on technical descents; anterior knee pain and quad soreness from descending; Achilles and calf from steep climbing.

## Prevention
- Ankle balance training: about 35% fewer sprains, most in those with a previous sprain (Schiftan 2015). Strong in other sports.
- Downhill tolerance: progressive downhill running exposure (Bontemps 2020, moderate); eccentric quad strength (reverse Nordic, slow step-downs, Spanish squat) is practice.
- Calf and soleus capacity: practice. Single-leg heel raise to fatigue: general population median about 25 reps (Sleeswijk Visser et al. 2025).
- Tibialis and ankle strength for rough ground: practice.

## Variants
- Short trail and fell (up to about 2 h, steep, technical): power, descending speed, ankle reactivity.
- Ultra (marathon plus): durability, eccentric quad tolerance, calf endurance, trunk endurance with a pack; gym volume kept low in big running blocks.
- Mountain and vertical (very steep, often with poles): hip and knee extensor strength, step-ups, some upper-body pulling for poles.

## Season (UK defaults, practice)
- Fell Runners Association and trail calendars run year round, with most races spring to autumn and winter fell races.
- Defaults: OFF Nov-Jan (gym 2, heaviest strength), PRE Feb-Apr (gym 2, more reactive and eccentric work), IN May-Oct (gym 1). For an A-race, use a 10-14 day taper.

## Tests (bands practice, anchored where stated)
- Single-leg heel raise to fatigue (median 25 general population).
- CMJ.
- Single-leg hop for distance asymmetry.
- Y-balance anterior reach difference (> 4 cm flag, Plisky 2006).
- Single-leg wall sit or isometric split squat hold time (practice).
