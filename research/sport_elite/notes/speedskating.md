# Speed skating: research note

## Demands
- Long track: 500 m to 10,000 m on a 400 m oval. Crouched posture with small knee and trunk angles is mechanically efficient but increases muscle deoxygenation; pacing balances a fast start against holding technique (Konings et al. 2015).
- Short track: 111 m oval, 500 to 1500 m (plus relays), pack racing with tactical surges, falls and contact.
- 800 m long track vs 666 m short track time trials in elite short-trackers: knee angle about 103 deg in both, trunk angle lower in short track; lactate 10.5 vs 11.0 mmol/L; end VO2 about 58-60 ml/kg/min. Short track felt harder for hours afterwards, suggesting longer recovery (Hettinga, Konings, Cooper 2016).
- Energy: the 500 m is largely anaerobic, 1000-1500 m mixed, 5000-10,000 m largely aerobic (general physiology; not directly verified in a source I opened).
- Inline speed skating: same crouched stride on wheels; track and road races including long road events. Little research found; use long track evidence plus practice.

## Injuries
- Scoping review: competition injury proportion 2-18% of participants; seasonal back pain prevalence up to 84%; one study linked short track injury to functional strength deficits, another linked long track injury to training load; no prevention study tested effectiveness; short track helmets performed worse than other sports' helmets (Hendricks, Verhagen, van de Water 2024).
- Dutch elite youth long track (84 skaters, one season): weekly health problem prevalence 30.5%; acute injury 2.0 per 1,000 h. Highest burdens: repetitive knee (11.0 days lost per 1,000 h), thoracic spine (6.8), lower leg (3.9), lumbosacral (2.5); acute head (5.6) and shoulder (2.9) (Hendricks et al. 2024, BJSM).
- German national short track team 2009-2020: 0.53 injuries per 1,000 h; 69.8% lower limb, mainly knee and ankle; 30% of injuries in off-ice training (Ziegler et al. 2025).
- Groin: across sports, greater adductor strength predicts less future groin pain (SMD -0.5); abductor strength and the ratio do not (BJSM 2024 meta-analysis). Relevant to the push-off.

## Prevention
- No skating prevention trial. Priorities (practice, informed by the burden data): lumbar and thoracic capacity, knee (patellar) load management, adductor strength (moderate evidence from other sports), hip abduction for the stride, head and neck protection for short track, sensible off-ice load (30% of short track injuries happened off ice).

## Variants
- Long track: sprinters (500-1000 m) need max strength and power; distance skaters need aerobic capacity and crouch endurance.
- Short track: more lateral and left-turn work, crossovers, falls, higher recovery cost.
- Inline: outdoor season, road races, hard-surface crashes.

## Season (UK, practice)
- No 400 m oval in the UK: long track skaters train abroad in winter. Short track runs roughly September to April. Inline runs spring to autumn outdoors. Cycling is the usual summer cross-training (practice).

## Tests with norms
- 30 s Wingate (Monark at 7.5% body mass), Polish national team skaters. Senior men peak power: average 12.40-13.33 W/kg, high 13.80-14.25, very high above 14.25. Senior women: average 10.66-11.41, very high above 12.16. Mean power senior men average 9.76-10.35 W/kg (Sitkowski et al. 2025).
- Standing broad jump, CMJ, lateral bound asymmetry, estimated squat 1RM: practice bands.

## Evidence limits
- Injury data improving but prevention evidence absent; strength work extrapolated. Confidence: moderate.
