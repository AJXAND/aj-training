# Karate: research note (kumite and kata)

## Demands
- Kumite (sparring): bouts of 2-3 min (3 min men, 2 min women under older WKF rules, as cited by the Middlesex S&C chapter). Rest between bouts from about 5 to 30 min in tournaments.
- Chaabene et al. 2014 (Asian J Sports Med 5(1):21-29), official bouts: high-intensity actions last 1.5 s; effort:rest 1:1.5 (10 s activity, 16 s pause); high-intensity:rest 1:11; blood lactate 11.1 mmol/L after official bouts (7.8 simulated). Lower-limb muscles rated most strained.
- Beneke et al. 2004 (as reported by Roschel 2009, JSSM): kumite about 78% aerobic, about 17% alactic; lactate rose about 6 mmol/L after a match. About 16 decisive actions and about 19 s of high-intensity work a match.
- Roschel et al. 2009 (JSSM 8 CSSI3:20-24), Brazilian national team: winners had about 23% more upper-limb and 11% more lower-limb power at 30% 1RM; no difference in 1RM or jump height. Fast, light-load power matters more than maximal strength.
- Doria et al. 2009 (Eur J Appl Physiol 107:603-610), world champions: kata lasts about 140-160 s; kumite energy cost about twice kata. VO2max, jump height and Wingate did not differ between kata and kumite athletes. Kumite relied mainly on aerobic energy. Kata has higher relative anaerobic share (exact split not in abstract).
- Koropanovski et al. 2011 (J Hum Kinet), national team: kumite athletes taller, heavier, faster over 10 m and better on standing triple jump; kata athletes slightly more flexible (not significant).
- CMJ: international karateka about 22% higher CMJ than national (Balsalobre-Fernandez 2013). Elite Brazilian mixed-sex group: CMJ 37.1 +/- 7.8 cm, SJ 34.2 cm (Spigolon 2018).

## Injuries
- Lystad et al. 2020 (BJSM 54(16):976), meta-analysis of 28 studies, Olympic-style kumite: 88.3 injuries per 1000 athlete-exposures, 39.2 per 1000 min (about one every 25 min of fighting). Head and neck median 57.9%. Contusions 68%, lacerations 19%, concussion 2%. Men slightly higher per exposure. Most injuries minor.
- Tokyo 2020 Olympics: karate 19 injuries per 100 athletes, about double the Games average of 9.1 (Soligard et al. 2023, BJSM).
- Severe knee injury: 24.2% of 293 athletes at the 2018 World Championships reported one in their career; commonest mechanism was pivoting or dodging (36-40%) (J Sports Med Phys Fitness 2021). Kumite athletes had more in competition than training.
- Kata: no injury epidemiology found. Deep stances and repeated low-stance transitions load the knee and hip (practice).
- Prevention trials in karate: none found. Head injury is reduced by rules and equipment, not gym work. Gym prevention is borrowed: landing and cutting (knee), ankle balance, Nordic (kick deceleration), hip flexor and adductor capacity for kicking (practice).

## Variants
- Kumite: repeated 1.5 s explosive actions, footwork, lateral and back steps, head contact. Priorities: fast light-load power, single-leg reactive strength, repeat-effort fitness, knee and ankle prevention.
- Kata: 1.5-3 min continuous, deep stances held under control, high hip range, explosive techniques with sharp stops (kime). Priorities: isometric and eccentric strength in deep stance positions, hip mobility under load, trunk stiffness, single-leg balance.

## Season (UK, practice)
- Competitions run most of the year (national and regional opens, national championships). Plan around 2-3 key tournaments: 8-12 week build, 4-6 week specific block, 7-10 day taper.
- Weight-category athletes: no dehydration cuts for amateurs.

## Tests
- CMJ (elite pooled 37 cm; bands practice).
- 10 m sprint or 5 m lunge-step speed (kumite).
- Yo-Yo IR1 or Karate Specific Aerobic Test (KSAT, reliable: ICC > 0.90, smallest real change 9.4%: Chaabene 2012, Biol Sport). KSAT needs its audio protocol, so the app uses Yo-Yo IR1.
- Single-leg balance or Y-balance.
- Side split or hip abduction range (kata).
